Fuel Cell

In order to use green hydrogen as an energy source, its chemical energy must be converted into electrical energy. Fuel cells are used for this purpose; their function, structure, and types are explained below.



What is a fuel cell?

A fuel cell converts the chemical energy of hydrogen directly into electrical energy and heat. The reaction between hydrogen and oxygen in the fuel cell enable production of emission-free electricity, as the main byproduct of the reaction is water.



Structure of a fuel cell

A fuel cell consists of a group of several cells that are combined into what is known as a stack. Each cell contains an anode, a cathode, and an electrolyte between the electrodes. Catalysts and diffusion layers additionaly ensure thtat the reaction gases reach the electrodes.



Function of a fuel cell

1. Hydrogen is fed to the anode.

2. There, it is split into protons and electrons.

3. The electrons released in this process go through an external circuit generating an electric current.

4. In the electrolyte, protons migrate toward the cathode.

5. At the cathode, protons and electrons combine with oxygen creating water.

There are currently six different types of fuel cells differing in terms of electrolytes used and operating temperature. These 6 types of fuel cells are divided into two categories: low-temperature fuel cells and medium- and high-temperature fuell cells. The alkaline fuell cell (AFC), the membrane fuell cell (PEM) and the direct-methanol fuel cell (DMFC) belong to low-temperature fuel cells. The phosporic acid fuell cell (PAFC), the molten carbonate fuell cell (MCFC) and the solid oxide fuel cell (SOFC) are classified as medium- and high-temperature fuel cells.



Since the technology continuously evolves, it is reasonable to assume that the fuel cell's importance will increase in areas such as energy supply and mobility.
