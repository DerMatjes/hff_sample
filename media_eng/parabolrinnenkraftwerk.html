Parabolic trough power plants

Translated from a technical paper by Mathias Lief.

Solar energy is an ideal energy source for generating electricity and hydrogen without the use of fossil fuels. In sunny regions, parabolic trough power plants offer an efficient way to harness these enormous amounts of energy for the production of electricity or for the electrolysis of hydrogen.

How it works

A parabolic trough power plant is a concentrating solar power plant. This means it uses parabolic mirrors to concentrate the sun’s rays onto a long absorber tube. This tube is filled with a fluid (the heat transfer medium), which absorbs the energy of the rays in the form of heat. This heat is then transported further to be used for generating electricity. To ensure that the power plant can continue to generate electricity at night, a thermal storage system is required. During periods of strong solar radiation and low electricity demand, this system stores a portion of the heat contained in the heat transfer fluid so that it can be released again as needed for electricity production.

The absorbed heat is transferred to a second steam cycle via heat exchangers (preheaters, evaporators, superheaters, and intermediate superheaters). The resulting steam then drives a turbine, which generates electricity with the help of a generator. To absorb as much heat as possible, the steam is cooled until it returns to a liquid state.

Energy yield

The amount of energy yield depends on the overall efficiency of the system. Energy losses occur during concentration on the absorber, during heat transfer, and during conversion to electricity. The efficiency of an efficient power plant for electricity generation is about 50%; when storing energy in hydrogen, about 10% of the total efficiency is lost compared to that of electricity. In a hypothetical—but realistic—plant covering an area of 600,000 m² (corresponding to a square with an edge length of 775 m), approximately 160,000 m² is used as a mirror surface. Based on an annual average solar irradiance (including nights) of 500 W/m², this results inthe electrical output.

\[P_{electr} = {160\ 000 \ m^2 \cdot 500 \ \frac {W}{m^2} \ \cdot 0,5 }\] \[ = 4 \cdot 10^7 \ W\](40 000 000 Watt)

Over the course of one year (\(3,1 \cdot 10^7s\)) the energy yield is:

\(E_{electr} = 4 \cdot 10^7 \ W\cdot 3,1 \cdot 10^7 \ s\) \[= 1,3 \cdot 10^{15} \ J \]\[=360 \cdot 10^6 \ kWh \](360 000 000 kWh)

Similarly, based on an annual energy demand of 3,500 kWh, this area could supply approximately 100,000 households with electricity.
