The Hydrogen combustion engine

The hydrogen combustion engine is considered a promising addition to battery-electric and fuel cell-based engines. The basic principle is similar to that of a conventional combustion engine, but hydrogen is used as fuel instead of diesel or gasoline. The combustion process produces mainly water vapor, while only small amounts of nitrogen oxides (NOx) are emitted. The technology is by no means new: the first hydrogen engines were developed as early as 1807 by the French officer François Isaac de Rivaz.[1]

The meta-study by NOW GmbH shows that the technical functionality of the hydrogen engine has now been clearly demonstrated. Particularly advantageous are its high robustness, the use of existing engine technology, and its suitability for applications with high performance requirements. In addition, modern hydrogen engines achieve efficiencies of over 40 percent and are already considered an alternative to diesel engines in some areas. The study concludes that hydrogen engines and fuel cells are likely to coexist in the future. Heavy-duty commercial vehicles, construction and agricultural machinery, and applications in harsh environmental conditions benefit most from the characteristics of the hydrogen engine.[1]

The industry is also contributing to this development. MAN has been developing hydrogen engines for commercial vehicles and stationary applications for years. The company sees significant potential, particularly in logistics, agriculture, and off-road applications. Hydrogen can help make internal combustion engines nearly climate-neutral, as long as the hydrogen is produced from renewable energy sources.[2]

Another key player is DEUTZ. The company launched a hydrogen engine, the TCG 7.8 H₂, back in 2021 and now considers the technology to be ready for the market. The engine meets European requirements for “zero-emission” applications and demonstrates that hydrogen engines are no longer just research projects, but are already real products.[3]

The technology is also being extensively tested in motorsports. Toyota has been developing hydrogen combustion engines for several years and unveiled the “GR LH2 Racing Concept” prototype for the 24 Hours of Le Mans. The vehicle uses a hydrogen engine in combination with a hybrid system and serves as a test platform for further development of hydrogen technologies. Toyota aims to demonstrate that climate-friendly motorsports are possible even with combustion engines.[4]

Current state-of-the-art technology shows that hydrogen combustion engines have a promising future, particularly regarding the commercial vehicle sector. While fuel cells are primarily valued for their high efficiency, hydrogen engines stand out for their robustness and their ability to utilize existing production and engine technology. As a result, they are viewed as a key component of future climate-friendly mobility powered by green hydrogen.
